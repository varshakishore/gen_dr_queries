  1. [1] Opacity as a Feature, Not a Flaw: The LoBOX Governance Ethic for Role-Sensitive Explainability and Institutional Trust in AI
    F. Herrera, R. Calder'on
    The metaphor of the "black box" has become the dominant topic in public and academic discourse on artificial intelligence (AI) (Castelvecchi, 2016), reflecting the discomfort with systems whose internal workings resist scrutiny and accountability. Transparency is often framed as the antidote: if we could open the box and see how decisions are made, AI could be rendered fair, safe, and trustworthy. Yet, this perspective, however well-intentioned, rests on a flawed assumption: that transparency is always feasible and ethically sufficient. Explainable AI (XAI) has emerged as the central transparence field tasked with making opaque systems intelligible and accountable. Initially focused on technical transparency and model interpretability, XAI has evolved into a governance-oriented discipline that bridges the cognitive complexity of AI with the ethical and legal responsibilities of its users. As Arrieta et al. (2020) (Arrieta et al., 2020) emphasize, XAI is essential to foster trust and responsible Ai, based on two central concepts: understanding and audience. However, as Herrera (2025) (Herrera, 2025) notes, explainability must go beyond surface-level transparency and embrace its relational and contextual dimensions, including support for human-AI collaboration. Within this evolving landscape, Role-Sensitive Explainability has emerged as a foundational principle. It recognizes that not all users need the same kind of explanation: explanations must be tailored to the cognitive needs, stakes, and institutional roles of diverse stakeholders, from developers and regulators to experts and lay users. This shift repositions XAI as a socio-technical bridge rather than a generic toolkit, one that connects AI systems to their contexts of use through adaptive, audience-calibrated strategies. The RED / BLUE XAI approach -Research, Explore, Debug, and responsiBle, Legal, trUst, Ethics -XAI (Biecek & Samek, 2024) reflects this paradigm. RED XAI supports developers and auditors through technical fidelity and diagnostic transparency, while BLUE XAI provides lay users and decision makers with accessible, ethically grounded justifications (Biecek & Samek, 2024;Herrera, 2025).

  3. [3] Passengers in Flight: AI Governance Capacity in the Global South
    Sami Farhad
    As AI develops, decision-making is increasingly carried out by algorithms, rather than by human beings. The algorithms may lower costs, improve productivity or enable new business models, but also raise important questions about responsibility, discrimination, fairness and transparency. One important part of the response to these concerns, which also can enhance national AI governance capacity in Global South countries, is algorithmic transparency (sometimes called algorithmic explainability). Algorithmic transparency is "the principle that the factors that influence the decisions made by algorithms should be visible, or transparent, to the people who use, regulate, and are affected" by them (Maillé & Tuffin, 2022, 143, quoting Wikipedia). This is not a requirement that companies share their proprietary source code, which would disincentivize investment from the AI Core and place technical burdens on government staff reviewing and securing the code. Rather, it is the reasonable expectation from society that those who deploy AI should provide an appropriate degree of explanation as to how the AI produces its outputs. Algorithmic transparency lays the foundation for users, civil society and government to test the explanations provided in light of observed outcomes, community values and public policy. National policies of a Global South country on algorithmic transparency should seek to address real outcomes from the use of AI in the country. Focusing on actual ex poste local outcomes, rather than ex ante review can stimulate evidence-based learning by government departments, the media and civil society, as well as information-sharing by affected citizens. This can help narrow the information asymmetry between AI Core technology providers and government (Taeihagh, 2021) and lessen the risk of stakeholders accepting, without question, an overly-sunny "innovation narrative" (Kilic, 2024, 1) from AI Core actors. Importantly, algorithmic transparency also aids government and other stakeholders in Global South countries to better understand that, regardless of industry-adopted ethics statements or codes of conduct, an often different set of values and priorities may be embedded in detailed technical design choices of specific AI products and services, the so-called "principles-topractices gap" (Palladino, 2022, 2). An example can demonstrate the utility of this approach. Food delivery apps, as multi-sided platforms, structure an economic relationship among their customers, restaurants and delivery riders.
