Conclusion in brief
- It is reasonable to state that theta–gamma coupling reflects coordination between control-related fronto-parietal systems and limbic/hippocampal structures during memory-intensive tasks, and that this coordination supports the temporal organization of spatial information consistent with establishing and maintaining cognitive maps and integrating new items into them, although much of this linkage is inferred from task-dependent coupling dynamics rather than a single study that uses the exact phrasing.<cite id=4540820d-3,4540820d-6,39a34c8f-2,4540820d-2,59cf6f11-3,59cf6f11-6,59cf6f11-7,0f884bd0-2,0f884bd0-4,39a34c8f-5,39a34c8f-6,39a34c8f-7,4540820d-8,59cf6f11-9>

What the evidence shows about large-scale network coordination
- Limbic structures, especially the hippocampus, show prominent engagement with theta rhythms and long-range interactions with neocortex during memory processes, consistent with a role in coordinating distributed systems that support episodic and spatial functions.<cite id=59cf6f11-3> Accumulating evidence links theta-band synchrony to hippocampal coding of events (past, present, and future) and to long-distance communication with neocortex, and human work shows theta-mediated coordination between prefrontal and temporoparietal DMN nodes.<cite id=59cf6f11-3> Cross-network interactions outside the default mode are dominated by higher frequencies (theta to gamma), indicating that information exchange between DMN-related and other networks is organized by phase-amplitude coupling mechanisms.<cite id=59cf6f11-6> Resting-state analyses also implicate ascending synchronizing systems, medial temporal lobe, and DMN in coordinated theta coupling, further supporting a network-level orchestration of memory-related systems by theta.<cite id=59cf6f11-7> Moreover, the strength of large-scale network coupling (e.g., with the salience network) relates to working memory performance, underscoring functional interaction between control and memory systems during attention-demanding tasks.<cite id=59cf6f11-9>

Theta–gamma coupling between hippocampus and prefrontal/ECN regions
- Intraoperative and scalp studies converge on a mechanism in which theta cycles coordinate distributed gamma activity across hippocampus and cortex to maintain multiple items over time and to organize sequences; coupling between hippocampal theta and cortical gamma increases with working memory load and is modulated by the number of items to be maintained.<cite id=39a34c8f-2,4540820d-2> Animal work shows that hippocampal–prefrontal theta–slow gamma coupling is elevated on successful trials and increases with task difficulty, and that such coupling tracks synchronized activity within prefrontal neurons, consistent with coordinated maintenance of spatial information.<cite id=39a34c8f-5,39a34c8f-6> Human intracranial recordings during navigation similarly reveal that hippocampal theta power and hippocampal–entorhinal theta–gamma dynamics support planning and retrieval of sequential spatial information, indicating that theta–gamma coupling helps organize upcoming actions and access stored routes.<cite id=4540820d-3,4540820d-6,4540820d-8>

Link to cognitive maps and placement of new items
- The hippocampus is widely regarded as the neural substrate for cognitive maps—allocentric representations of space and trajectories—that guide navigation and memory, with lesion and imaging evidence in primates and humans emphasizing allocentric spatial knowledge and scene processing.<cite id=0f884bd0-2,0f884bd0-4> The same hippocampal–prefrontal interactions that exhibit task-dependent theta–gamma coupling are the anatomical and functional substrate for encoding new spatial information and maintaining/manipulating task-relevant representations during working memory, which can be interpreted as placing new items onto an existing cognitive map in service of goal-directed behavior.<cite id=0f884bd0-3,0f884bd0-9,4540820d-6,4540820d-8> Critically, human hippocampal theta–gamma coupling coordinates sequential planning in abstract state spaces, with hippocampal theta coding distance to a goal and coupling patterns reflecting whether a path is newly encoded or previously retrieved, consistent with temporal scaffolding that supports adding new episodes and integrating them into a map.<cite id=4540820d-3,4540820d-6>

Putting it together: Is the claim reasonable?
- Yes, it is reasonable. The literature supports that: (1) limbic (hippocampal) and control (fronto-parietal/ECN) networks coordinate during memory-intensive tasks; (2) this coordination is expressed in oscillatory coupling—particularly theta–gamma—between hippocampus and prefrontal regions that scales with memory demands; and (3) these coupling dynamics are engaged when spatial sequences are planned and retrieved, aligning with the need to establish/maintain cognitive maps and to incorporate new items into them for guiding attention and action.<cite id=59cf6f11-3,59cf6f11-6,39a34c8f-2,4540820d-2,4540820d-3,4540820d-6,0f884bd0-2,0f884bd0-4,39a34c8f-5,39a34c8f-6,59cf6f11-7,4540820d-8>

Important caveats
- Oscillatory coupling is heterogeneous (phase–amplitude, phase–phase, and frequency-specific effects), and the direction of influence (hippocampus→prefrontal vs. prefrontal→hippocampus) can depend on phase, task phase (encoding vs. retrieval), and frequency band; much of the strongest evidence in humans is correlational and often derived from intracranial or high-sensitivity EEG/MEG settings.<cite id=39a34c8f-2,4540820d-2,4540820d-6,4540820d-8,59cf6f11-6> The explicit phrase “placing representations of items of attention within cognitive maps” is not directly used in the retrieved studies; instead, the coupling evidence supports temporal coordination and integration of spatial information during planning and retrieval.<cite id=4540820d-3,4540820d-6,4540820d-8> Nevertheless, taken together, the converging evidence supports the core claim that theta–gamma coupling between control and limbic networks underpins the coordination needed to build and use cognitive maps and to integrate new information into them.

## Short answer
Partly yes: there is strong mechanistic evidence that hippocampal–prefrontal coordination during spatial memory relies on theta–gamma coupling, and large-scale network studies show the executive control network (dorsal frontoparietal) dynamically couples with limbic/hippocampal systems during cognitively demanding, memory-intensive tasks. However, direct human evidence that theta–gamma coupling is the specific mechanism by which the ECN and limbic RSN coordinate "to maintain cognitive maps" is indirect; the best-supported claim is that theta–gamma coupling coordinates hippocampus–prefrontal communication during spatial working memory and learning, which are processes that constitute the maintenance and updating of cognitive maps.[?]

## Limitations and what would resolve them
- The clearest mechanistic demonstrations of theta–gamma coupling coordinating hippocampal–prefrontal communication come from rodent LFP recordings and reviews; direct human hippocampal–prefrontal theta–gamma coupling tied to "cognitive map maintenance" per se is less directly measured in the retrieved evidence. - To firmly answer "yes," one would need human hippocampal–prefrontal recordings (or high-resolution fMRI/bold oscillatory tagging) showing task-dependent theta–gamma coupling during navigation/working memory that predicts maintenance fidelity of cognitive maps, ideally with causal tests (e.g., tACS entrainment) and dissociations when coupling is perturbed.

## Bottom line
- Mechanistically, theta–gamma coupling plausibly provides the oscillatory code that coordinates hippocampus with prefrontal control systems during spatial memory, a core component of maintaining cognitive maps.[?]
- At the network level, ECN and limbic systems do coordinate during memory-intensive tasks, supporting the functional plausibility that theta–gamma coupling could be the mechanism by which they coordinate map maintenance.[?]
- Therefore, while the evidence strongly supports a role for theta–gamma coupling in hippocampal–prefrontal coordination during spatial memory, the specific claim that it is the mechanism by which the ECN and limbic RSN coordinate "to maintain cognitive maps" in humans remains best supported as plausible but not definitively proven in the retrieved literature.[?]
