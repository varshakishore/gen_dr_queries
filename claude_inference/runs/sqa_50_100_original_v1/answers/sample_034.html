# Overview

Historically, animal testing for endocrine disruption relied on coordinated in vivo screening and apical study batteries developed through the U.S. EPA Endocrine Disruptor Screening Program (EDSP) and the OECD Test Guidelines Programme, alongside fish and amphibian assays targeting the endocrine pathways (EPA, 2013a; OECD, 2013a, b). These frameworks established Level 3 screening assays (rodent uterotrophic for estrogens and Hershberger for androgens), Level 4 short-term repeated-dose studies, and Level 5 full reproductive/developmental toxicity studies, with endpoints sensitive to endocrine activity including organ weights, serum hormones, and reproductive endpoints.     Alterations in hormonal homeostasis from possible interactions between chemical exposures and the estrogen receptor have been the focus of extensive toxicologic research and regulatory investigation. For example, the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) has been engaged in developing the Endocrine Disruptor Screening Program (EDSP) for more than a decade. Under the EDSP, EPA has developed a Tier 1 screening battery (EDSP-ESB) consisting of 11 different assays, including both in vitro and in vivo methods, for screening chemicals to identify substances with the potential to interact with components of the estrogen (E), androgen (A), and thyroid (T) systems (EPA, 2013a). Through a global collaborative effort, complementary test guidelines for endocrine screening assays have also been developed by the Organization for Economic Cooperation and Development (OECD, 2013a(OECD, ,2013b)). While the endpoints in the EDSP-ESB and the corresponding OECD test guidelines are not considered to be adverse effects, these assays have been developed to be highly sensitive to detect interactions of chemicals with components of the EAT pathways. Compared with in vitro assays, the in vivo EDSP-ESB assays have the advantage that they include in vivo physiologic systems, integrated biologic responses, and intact in vivo metabolic systems, including intestinal and firstpass metabolism (Borgert et al., 2003;Charles et al., 2007). Among the in vivo EDSP-ESB assays, the rat uterotrophic assay has proven in the joint OECD-EPA validation studies to be a reliable and reproducible test, and this assay allows for a comparison across selected compounds of relative estrogenic potency on an administered dose basis (Kanno et al., 2001(Kanno et al., , 2003)). However, as noted above, this assay has been optimized as a screening-level method, and does not provide information of the potential health effects, if any, of exposure to chemicals at low, environmentally relevant levels.

    The gold standard for the identification and assessment of deleterious effects of prenatal exposure to chemicals on developing organisms is the OECD TG 414: Prenatal developmental toxicity study [14]. This TG has equivalents in protocol 870.3700 for USEPA and protocol B.31 for European regulations [16]. The assessment of developmental toxicity via OECD TG 414 is required for authorization of use of a lot of chemicals that human beings are going to be exposed to. Indeed, the REACH regulation demands that the OECD TG 414 test should be performed in at least one species for all substances with a tonnage band higher than 100 tons/year. Moreover, other substances outside REACH regulation, such as biocides or plant protection products, also require this developmental toxicity test. Although other OECD TGs include testing for developmental toxicity, such as the OECD TG 421: Reproduction/developmental toxicity screening test [17] and the OECD TG 422: Combined repeated dose toxicity study with reproduction/developmental toxicity screening test [16,18], they could only be relevant in terms of preliminary hazard identification and for setting priorities for further test requirements but not for detailed and reliable risk assessment due to their limited ability to unmask teratogenicity [19]. The main animal species used in the TG 414 is rat (Rattus norvegicus) in case of rodents and rabbit (Oryctolagus cuniculus) in case of non-rodents. Nevertheless, other animal species can be occasionally used when supported by robust scientific reasons, as is the case of mouse (Mus musculus) or Guinea pig (Cavia Porcellus). The current version of TG 414 was launched in June 2018 when some endpoints for detecting EDCs were added, namely the anogenital distance (AGD) in fetuses and thyroid hormones in dams. Indeed, these are the only requested observations linked to a MoA, even if they are mandatory only for rodents but not for rabbits.   3. [3] Regulatory integration of new approach methodologies for human-relevant developmental and reproductive toxicity (DART) assessment: A systematic cross-sector review
    S. Kumbhar, Shrikant N Borude, Rahil Deshmukh
    To address inconsistencies in experimental design and improve the comparability of toxicological data, international organizations began developing standardized testing guidelines for reproductive and developmental toxicity assessment. Among the most influential initiatives was the establishment of the test guideline program by the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD), which aimed to harmonize toxicological testing procedures across member countries [45]. The OECD Test Guidelines provide detailed protocols describing study design, animal selection, dosing regimens, and evaluation endpoints for a wide range of toxicological assessments. Within the field of reproductive toxicology, several guidelines were introduced to standardize the evaluation of fertility and developmental toxicity. For example, OECD Test Guideline 414 focuses on prenatal developmental toxicity and assesses fetal growth, viability, and structural abnormalities following maternal exposure to a test substance during pregnancy. Similarly, OECD Test Guidelines 421 and 422 provide screening approaches for evaluating reproductive and developmental effects within a combined study design [46]. The implementation of standardized guidelines significantly improved the reproducibility and regulatory acceptance of reproductive toxicity studies. Furthermore, the OECD framework for Mutual Acceptance of Data (MAD) enabled toxicity data generated in one member country to be accepted by regulatory authorities in others, reducing duplication of studies and facilitating international regulatory harmonization [45,46]. In addition to core reproductive toxicity guidelines, OECD has expanded testing strategies to include specialized endpoints such as developmental neurotoxicity (DNT) and endocrine disruption. Test guidelines such as OECD TG 426 (developmental neurotoxicity) and internationally coordinated endocrine disruptor screening programs reflect increasing regulatory attention toward complex and mechanistically driven developmental outcomes, further highlighting the limitations of traditional apical testing approaches and the need for complementary NAM-based strategies.
