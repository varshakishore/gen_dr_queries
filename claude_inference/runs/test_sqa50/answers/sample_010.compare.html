This is a deep, multi-layered question that sits at the intersection of historiography, political sociology, and political economy. The answer requires mapping the blind spots of each major tradition — colonial/orientalist, nationalist-secular, Marxist, Subaltern Studies, and Hindutva — and then identifying the causal mechanisms that fall into the gaps *between* them. Let me proceed systematically.

# Causal Mechanisms Systematically Underweighted in Explaining Muslim Marginalization in India

## I. The Major Historiographic Traditions and Their Constitutive Blind Spots

### 1. Colonial/Orientalist Historiography

The colonial historiographic tradition, associated with figures like James Mill and Henry Elliot, created the foundational framework that all subsequent traditions either accepted or reacted against. Colonial administrators like James Mill and Henry Elliot manufactured a bifurcated history of India, dividing it into a "Golden Age" Hindu past and a "dark age" of Muslim despotism to legitimize their rule. ### 2. Nationalist/Secular Historiography

The secular-nationalist school (associated with Nehru, Tara Chand, and later Bipan Chandra) correctly identified communalism as a product of colonialism and rejected the colonial binary. However, nationalist historians ignored or severely underplayed inner contradictions of Indian society based on class and caste or the oppression of and discrimination against women and tribes. They have also ignored the movements against class and caste oppressions. They have seldom made an in-depth analysis of the national movement, and often indulged in its blind glorification. While adopting a secular position and condemning communalism, they do not make a serious analysis of its character or elements, causation, and development. ### 3. Marxist Historiography

The Marxist school (Irfan Habib, Romila Thapar, Bipan Chandra) correctly diagnosed colonialism's role in generating economic stagnation and class competition. The Marxist perspective views communalism as a response to the uneven development caused by colonialism, leading to competition for jobs and the mobilization of group identities. The Hindutva tradition inverts the causality of marginalization entirely. Hindu nationalism's ideology is built on a colonial-era history that falsely framed India's past as a perpetual Hindu-Muslim conflict, a distorted narrative now weaponized to fuel majoritarian politics. By embracing this colonial framework, Hindu nationalism continues to weaponize a distorted history to mobilize fear, hatred, and majoritarian pride. ## II. Causal Mechanisms Systematically Underweighted Across *All* Major Traditions

Here lies the most analytically important finding: despite their profound ideological differences, all five traditions share certain lacunae. Below are the mechanisms that fall through every historiographic net.

### Mechanism 4: Communal Violence as an Economic Mechanism, Not Just a Political One

These Muslim ghettos often lack basic facilities such as schools, hospitals, paved roads, clean drinking water, and sanitation. Additionally, ghettoization limits social interaction between different communities, fostering insularity. ## III. A Meta-Level Explanation: Why These Mechanisms Are Jointly Underweighted

The deeper structural reason all these mechanisms are underweighted is that every major tradition is locked into a **communalism-vs.-secularism binary** that was itself a colonial construct. As the research shows, the politicisation of the colonial public sphere affected the relations among the Indian Muslims in a new milieu. These arguments are significant in terms of rewriting the existing historiography that reinforces the binaries of nationalist–communalist or Hindu–Muslim politics. As long as the master question is "Were Muslims victims of Hindu communalism or agents of their own communalism?", the following causally significant phenomena remain largely invisible:

1. **Intra-community hierarchy** (who speaks for "Muslims")
2. **Occupation-specific economic devastation** (not all Indians lost equally from deindustrialization)
3. **The compounding geography of riot-driven segregation** (ghettos as recursive poverty traps)
4. **Institutional exclusion from the state apparatus** (civil service, military, judiciary)
5. **The political economy of Muslim leadership** (Ashraf elites extracting symbolic concessions while material deprivation deepens)

Each of these mechanisms operates *orthogonally* to the Hindu-Muslim axis. They require frameworks that are simultaneously class-analytic, institutionalist, spatially sensitive, and willing to distinguish between Muslim elite interests and Muslim mass interests. No single historiographic tradition has developed all of these capacities simultaneously — and that is precisely why they remain underweighted.
