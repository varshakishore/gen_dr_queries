    Aleksandar Nikodinovski, Darjan Karabašević, Vuk Mirčetić
    The accelerated adoption of digital workplace surveillance, particularly in remote work contexts, has created an urgent need to understand its psychological impacts on employees. While existing empirical research has documented negative effects of monitoring on worker well-being, trust, and performance, this literature exhibits a critical limitation: it derives predominantly from low-autonomy occupational contexts such as call centers, warehouses, and routine service work. High-autonomy knowledge workers (software developers, consultants, researchers, and creative professionals) remain systematically underexamined despite representing a substantial and growing segment of the workforce. This research gap is not merely academic; it has profound practical implications as organizations increasingly implement surveillance technologies across all job types without understanding how monitoring interacts with fundamental differences in work design and occupational norms. This study addresses this gap by developing the Autonomy-Surveillance Conceptual Framework, which systematically integrates Foucault's theory of disciplinary power, Nissenbaum's contextual integrity framework, and job design theory to explain differential surveillance impacts. Our core theoretical contribution is identifying job autonomy as a structural moderator that determines the severity of contextual integrity violations created by surveillance. For high-autonomy workers, surveillance constitutes a severe violation operating through three parallel mechanisms: trust erosion (as monitoring signals organizational distrust), procedural injustice (contradicting participatory norms), and temporal autonomy loss (constraining discretion over work timing). These mechanisms converge to produce strong negative outcomes, including reduced satisfaction, increased stress, and diminished commitment. Conversely, for low-autonomy workers, surveillance represents a moderate contextual integrity violation mediated primarily by fairness evaluations: workers assess whether monitoring is transparent, consistently applied, and legitimately purposed.
