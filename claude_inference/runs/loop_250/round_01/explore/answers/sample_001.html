# Rotator Cuff Tear: A Practical, Evidence‑Backed Overview

## What is the rotator cuff and why it tears
The rotator cuff is a group of tendons and muscles (supraspinatus, infraspinatus, teres minor, and subscapularis) that stabilize the humeral head and enable glenohumeral stability with shoulder motion. Full‑thickness tears completely detach tendon from bone, whereas partial‑thickness tears involve focal delamination or fibrillation without complete loss of continuity. Over decades, degeneration from aging, overuse, impingement, and intrinsic tendon changes increase risk of microtears that can progress, especially with tensile overload or trauma. ## Rehabilitation and return to function
Rehabilitation is time‑sensitive and evidence‑based. Acute surgical cases require postoperative protected motion to protect the healing footprint, followed by progressive strengthening of rotator cuff and scapular stabilizers, with timelines typically spanning 6–12 weeks for early motion and 3–6 months for strengthening, extending longer in large or failed repairs. Constant/Stener protocols and cuff‑focused home programs are standard in arthroscopic cases; compliance, pain control, and neuromuscular re‑education are key to preventing stiffness and weakness. In nonoperative care, supervised therapy emphasizing scapular plane mobility, rotator cuff endurance, and eccentric loading improves pain and function.
