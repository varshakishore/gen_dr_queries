Summary and definition of “effectiveness”
Across coral transplantation, “effective” artificial substrates are those that maximize survival and growth of transplanted fragments or settlers, accelerate time-to-coverage, support compatible community assembly, and remain structurally and chemically stable under site conditions over intended deployment durations. Effectiveness is context-dependent: species-specific larval settlement cues and preferences, local hydrodynamics and sedimentation, cost, scalability, and husbandry logistics all shape performance and suitability. Different approaches, like those used to recover damaged forests, have been attempted to rehabilitate degraded coral reefs over the past few decades abbreviated as "DT") involves attaching coral fragments from a donor to a recipient reef, and these are either attached to artificial structures (Fadli et al. 2012;Williams et al. 2019) or directly transplanted to the substrate (Forrester et al. 2012;Ladd et al. 2019;Lohr et al. 2017). Artificial structures are commonly used to increase coral cover by being deployed in damaged areas with abundant unconsolidated substrate, such as rubble patches (Fadli 2009;Ceccarelli et al. 2020). The use of concrete structures has emerged aiming at either combining substrate stabilization with the provision of habitat structure or providing structure only (Ceccarelli et al. 2020;Razak et al. 2022). When performing a DT approach, it is crucial to use durable materials since they will become a part of the reef (Bowden-Kerby 1997;Lindahl 2003;Dizon et al. 2008). In areas subjected to extreme weather events, it is particularly important to use materials adapted to these conditions. Moreover, applying DT practices on stable substrates can accelerate their self-attachment rates, thus increasing their survival rates (Bowden-Kerby 1997; Edwards and Clark 1999;Lindahl 2003;Dizon and Yap 2006).

This bacterial biofilm creates a suitable substrate for the attachment of larger organisms such as oysters, barnacles and other macro-fouling organisms as well as supplies an important food source for grazing invertebrates (Huggett et al., 2009). Concrete has been found to offer a favorable substrate for the establishment of organisms with calcareous skeletons, such as oysters, barnacles and corals, which deposit calcium carbonate onto the concrete surface as result of their physiological activities, being more important as they grow and develop (Risinger, 2012).
