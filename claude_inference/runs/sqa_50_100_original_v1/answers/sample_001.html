# Summary

Explainable AI is not "just a technical problem." It spans model design (interpretable-by-design vs. post‑hoc explanations), human factors, and organizational/governance requirements, with goals that often cannot be satisfied by algorithms alone.<cite id=d5af92c5-2>Interpretability and explainability are widely used interchangeably but lack universal agreement on meaning; interpretability is broader and includes explainability, with approaches ranging from inherently interpretable models to post‑hoc methods that generate explanations.</cite> <cite id=d5af92c5-0>Doshi‑Velez and Kim distinguish transparency (model structure) from explicability (post‑hoc explanations), and Miller's contrastive theory frames explanations of why an outcome occurred rather than how the model works.</cite> <cite id=d5af92c5-4>Popular taxonomies also emphasize diverse explanation problems (model vs. outcome explanation, inspection) and user needs across contexts, underscoring non‑technical, use‑case‑dependent goals.</cite>

# Why openness about training processes would not, by itself, solve XAI

- Technical limits of explanations mean more "openness" does not guarantee trustworthy or faithful insights. Post‑hoc explainers are often unstable/unfaithful and can be adversarially manipulated to hide discrimination while preserving predictions, undermining any purely technical disclosure of "explanations."<cite id=d5af92c5-5>Rudin argues post‑hoc explanations are often unreliable and can reflect correlations rather than computation; vulnerabilities of black‑box explanations have been documented.</cite> <cite id=d5af92c5-4>Popular post‑hoc tools (e.g., Grad‑CAM, LIME, SHAP) can diverge in non‑trivial ways, complicating selection and evaluation.</cite> <cite id=df7ac243-4>Sanity‑check studies show saliency maps can remain unchanged under randomization, giving users a false sense of security.</cite> <cite id=df7ac243-5>Explanations can be inconsistent/stable and adversarially attacked; they may also mislead users about fairness by appearing to align with protected‑attribute importance.</cite> <cite id=acceec27-8>With deep neural networks, the "black‑box" problem limits legal compliance because the model's decision rules are not easily interpretable to humans even if the rationale is disclosed.</cite>

- Human factors mean more documentation and code access do not automatically produce appropriate trust. Explanations can increase agreement with model outputs regardless of correctness, and may exacerbate overreliance and miscalibration without complementary safeguards.<cite id=df7ac243-2>Explanations can be interpreted as a signal of competence, increasing overreliance even when recommendations are wrong; passive explanations function as cognitive Band‑Aids.</cite> <cite id=df7ac243-0>Users may treat explanations with absolute confidence cues as trustworthy, increasing automation bias and reducing vigilance.</cite> <cite id=df7ac243-3>Post‑hoc "bi‑factual" explanations can lag behind model updates, mislead users, and add cognitive load.</cite> <cite id=df7ac243-6>Studies show explanations increase user agreement with outputs regardless of correctness; interventions like cognitive forcing can reduce overreliance but require design beyond "transparent" justifications.</cite>

- Adversaries can "game" openness. Attackers can modify models or training data to produce appearances of fairness or specific explanations while preserving predictions, showing that more disclosure alone is insufficient to prevent misuse.<cite id=9a1e3211-0>Adversarial "fairwashing" can create models that fool SHAP/LIME into showing the discriminatory feature as unimportant while predictions remain biased.</cite> <cite id=9a1e3211-1>Fairwashing can make ethical auditing less effective by presenting misleading explanations, creating a paradox where transparency tools are also exploited to avoid scrutiny.</cite> <cite id=df7ac243-8>Attackers can generate misleading explanations by producing scaffolding around a classifier that matches its classifications on training points but hides biases under local queries.</cite> <cite id=acceec27-6>Explanations can be manipulated (e.g., saliency map attacks) to yield arbitrary attributions without changing predictions, complicating detection.</cite> <cite id=9a1e3211-7>Fairwashed explanations can generalize across groups and transfer across models, making detection difficult in practice.</cite>

- Regulatory and governance obligations exceed model transparency. Laws already impose explanation/transparency duties, but they lack precise methodological guidance and must be interpreted in practice, indicating that openness at the training/process level is not sufficient without robust oversight, documentation, and accountability mechanisms.<cite id=acceec27-0>GDPR requires "meaningful information about the logic" for automated decisions, but lacks clear guidance on how such information should be conveyed.</cite> <cite id=acceec27-2>The EU AI Act requires transparency to enable users to interpret outputs and mandates human oversight and risk management, indicating that legal frameworks expect more than purely technical disclosure.</cite> <cite id=acceec27-5>The AI Act's transparency and human oversight provisions imply a need for explainability to enforce accountability, but details and enforceability remain debated.</cite> <cite id=acceec27-9>GDPR's fairness and transparency provisions have led to debates about whether they establish a right to technical interpretability, reflecting uncertainty about feasible methods.</cite>

# What would help: a socio‑technical approach

- Combine technical, organizational, and legal measures. Governance frameworks emphasize transparency, fairness audits, documentation, stakeholder inclusion, and accountability throughout the lifecycle; these are not purely algorithmic and often require processes, roles, and oversight beyond model disclosure.<cite id=bcf260da-1>Biased data, opaque algorithms, and deficient governance contribute to discriminatory outcomes; solutions include XAI, fair‑aware ML, and ethical governance frameworks across the lifecycle.</cite> <cite id=bcf260da-6>Technical fairness metrics risk a "framing trap" by decoupling component‑level properties from system‑level outcomes, highlighting the need for socio‑technical methods.</cite> <cite id=bcf260da-7>Effective AI fairness governance includes rules, processes, and roles across the AI lifecycle, with management support and oversight phases.</cite> <cite id=bcf260da-3>Transparency protocols, bias mitigation, and stakeholder inclusion are pillars of organizational alignment, but require design at the start of processes.</cite>

- Invest in inherently interpretable models or ante‑hoc architectures where appropriate, and pair post‑hoc methods with safeguards. Interpretable‑by‑design approaches can avoid some fidelity issues but often trade accuracy; organizations should select architectures and evaluation protocols to their needs and combine explanations with cognitive‑forcing, verification, and uncertainty communication.<cite id=d5af92c5-9>Interpretable models are transparent by nature but often sacrifice accuracy on complex data, while black‑box models require explanation methods; there is an ongoing trade‑off between interpretability and accuracy.</cite> <cite id=df7ac243-1>Post‑hoc explanations need not replicate internal reasoning to be helpful; risks can be mitigated via robust training, aggregation, and cognitive forcing to reduce overreliance.</cite>

# Bottom line

Explainable AI is fundamentally a cross‑disciplinary challenge: technical methods are necessary but not sufficient. Opening up training processes would help in some cases, but it cannot by itself solve core issues of faithfulness, robustness to manipulation, appropriate trust calibration, and organizational accountability; these require governance, audits, human‑centered design, and legal compliance mechanisms in addition to model‑level techniques.<cite id=d5af92c5-2>Interpretability and explainability lack universal definitions and span model design and communication.</cite> <cite id=d5af92c5-5>Technical limits of post‑hoc explanations are well‑documented, including unfaithfulness and vulnerabilities.</cite> <cite id=df7ac243-2>Explanations can increase overreliance, necessitating design safeguards beyond disclosure.</cite> <cite id=acceec27-0>Regulatory frameworks require meaningful information and transparency, but do not prescribe specific methods, underscoring non‑technical complexity.</cite> <cite id=9a1e3211-0>Adversarial fairwashing shows that more openness can be gamed to hide discrimination.</cite> <cite id=bcf260da-1>Combining technological, ethical, organizational, and regulatory actions across the AI lifecycle is needed for trustworthy AI.</cite>
