Bottom line

Static k values are one‑dimensional snapshots that ignore moisture sorption, vapor diffusion, phase change, and assembly‑level hygrothermal coupling. In hygroscopic insulations like hempcrete and straw bale, water uptake raises conductive heat transfer but the same materials also increase thermal capacity, buffer humidity and damp temperature swings, and maintain vapor permeability, all of which reduce peak heat loads and improve comfort beyond what static numbers predict. Consequently, using only static k in building‑physics design tends to underestimate wet‑season heat transfer and overestimate the “penalty” of hygroscopic insulation, while failing to capture its buffering and thermal‑mass benefits; hygrothermal characterization and dynamic modeling are required to reflect true in‑service performance. Summary

Hempcrete’s hygroscopic “moisture buffering” means its porous hemp hurd aggregate and lime binder adsorb and desorb water in response to ambient humidity, stabilizing wall RH and reducing the need for mechanical heating/cooling. This buffering arises from the high hygroscopic surface area and capillarity of hemp hurds and the chemically sorbing properties of lime, but it imposes material-process trade‑offs that increase embodied carbon relative to dense, low‑porosity conventional concrete. Mechanistically, buffering requires more absorbent and sorbent material (hemp hurds) and often higher lime dosages to fill capillary networks and ensure workability/set, while also increasing demands for protection against moisture-driven degradation; lime itself is energy‑intensive and only partially sequesters CO2 via carbonation, leaving a higher upstream footprint than conventional cement‑concrete assemblies. Despite this, hempcrete’s buffering can reduce operational energy and enable negative cradle‑to‑gate carbon over time in favorable conditions, so the net life‑cycle outcome depends on material choices, thickness/density, transport, and climate.
