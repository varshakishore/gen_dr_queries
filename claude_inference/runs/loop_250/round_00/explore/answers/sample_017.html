    The translational pipeline for CRISPR-engineered microbiome therapies in hematologic malignancies has accelerated remarkably, with pioneering studies demonstrating feasibility in complex mammalian systems and early-stage clinical trials exploring safety and biological activity. However, early human data remain limited, and the variability of outcomes across small trials mandates cautious interpretation until larger, randomized studies provide more robust efficacy and safety estimates. Leukemia mouse models incorporating CRISPR-edited commensals have provided compelling proof-of-concept for in situ tumor targeting. In one landmark study, researchers used lentiviral CRISPR-Cas9 systems to simultaneously disrupt up to five tumor suppressor genes, such as Tet2, Runx1, Dnmt3a, Nf1, and Ezh2, in murine HSCs, generating genetically complex AML models that faithfully recapitulate human disease heterogeneity. Building on early feasibility work, several groups have engineered commensal E. coli strains (including E. coli Nissle 1917) to secrete therapeutic single-domain antibodies or checkpoint-blocking fragments in situ. These reports validate the general strategy of bacterial in situ nanobody delivery; however, direct, peer-reviewed demonstrations of bacterial secretion of CD19-specific nanobodies in established lymphoma models are currently limited 94,95 . These models highlight the potential for CRISPR-edited commensals to overcome biological barriers that limit conventional therapies, particularly the accessibility of sanctuary sites like the bone marrow niche, where minimal residual disease persists 96 . However, it is important to recognize that murine immune systems and gut ecology differ substantially from humans, and therapeutic efficacy in mouse models often overestimates clinical benefit. Key limitations include differences in microbiota complexity, immune cell repertoire, and intestinal physiology, which together can mask human-specific safety signals and off-target effects. Future work should therefore incorporate humanized mouse models and early-phase translational studies to bridge this gap.   3. [3] Exploring the Potential of Biotics in Cancer Prevention and Treatment—Mechanisms, Experimental, and Clinical Insights
    Tia Tafla, Abinaya Balasubramanian, J. Iyer
    In recent years, there has been an increase in engineering microorganisms for a variety of therapeutic applications, and this concept is being applied to probiotics as well [170,171]. Some studies have engineered the intestinal probiotic Escherichia coli (E. coli) Nissle strain for therapeutic purposes. A study engineered E. coli Nissle 1917 to express p53, Tum-5, or both p53 and Tum-5 and administered these engineered probiotics to BALB/c mice bearing SMMC-7721 tumors. The engineered probiotics inhibited the expression of Ki67 while increasing the expression of caspase 3, resulting in significant inhibition of tumor growth in the animals [172]. E. coli Nissle 1917 has also been engineered to synthesize and release nanobodies in tumors that can bind to PD-L1 and CTLA4, proteins expressed by cancers to evade the immune system [173,174]. Mice treated with the engineered E. coli Nissle strain showed slower tumor growth and more presence of activated T-cells [174]. In another approach, E. coli Nissle has been engineered to synthesize and release cyclic dinucleotides in hypoxic conditions that can activate the STING pathway in immune cells, resulting in interferon production [175]. A phase I trial using this engineered E. coli strain, called SYNB1891, demonstrated that the microbe was safe to be injected alone or in combination with the chemotherapeutic atezolizumab [176]. In addition to the E. coli Nissle strain, Limosilactobacillus reuteri has been engineered to release the cytokine IL-22 in tumors. Mice with ovarian cancer that were treated with this engineered probiotic showed better survival and were able to undergo radiotherapy with better outcomes than the control group [177]. As time progresses, we expect to see more engineered probiotics for the treatment of cancer. While several studies claim the protective effects of different biotics as therapeutics, there are inconsistencies due to the complexity of carcinogenesis, dose-dependent and strain-dependent effects of probiotics, and safety concerns [178,179].
