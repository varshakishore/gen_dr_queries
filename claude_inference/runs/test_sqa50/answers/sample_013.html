Summary and framing
Emergency evacuations for 72-hour lead times hinge on whether life-threatening storm surge, winds, and inland flooding will arrive and how soon. Both typhoon track and intensity forecasts feed surge and wind hazard models, but their forecast uncertainties affect outcomes differently as lead time grows. Evidence from coupled atmospheric–surge modeling, operational track revision, and surge ensemble experiments shows that track forecast uncertainty typically dominates surge error, whereas intensity and wind-field size (RMW/size) are critical for local surge and hazard communication, especially near landfall and in narrow inlets and gulf/estuary settings.     ABSTRACT Hindcast experiments and pseudo-forecast experiments considering Typhoon Haishen (2020) were conducted using an atmospheric (WRF)-storm surge (GeoClaw) coupled model and a storm surge model with a parametric typhoon model. A series of simulations of the coupled model were used to quantify the error sources of the typhoon track and intensity in the forecast errors of storm surges. The results revealed that the typhoon track forecast had a larger error source for the storm surge forecast for the maximum surge height than the typhoon intensity. Mechanistic interpretation and caveats
Microwave imagers contribute by constraining upper‑tropospheric temperature/tropopause and moisture structures and by providing cloud/precipitation and wind information that corrects environmental steering and inner‑core thermodynamics, which propagate into intensity errors over 24–48 h. However, intensity forecasts are sensitive to landfall timing and storm motion errors; if track error accumulates, intensity error can persist even if microwave DA improved structural initialization. Thus, the isolated causal contribution is strongest when motion/track is well controlled and when inner‑core retrievals are available; in practice, this means microwave imagers reduce 48‑h intensity error by an amount that is case‑dependent but typically “tens of hPa in SLP and several m s−1 in maximum winds” over 24–48 h relative to controls, with the largest effects early and sustained benefits through 48 h when combined with other microwave products. What holds the value of “holding all other data constant”?
Experiments that toggle microwave imagers while holding GTS and model settings fixed (as in Chan‑Hom) provide the closest causal estimate. Limitations and what evidence would resolve them
Current evidence is mostly case‑based and multi‑sensor: experiments isolate microwave radiance/AMV but rarely factor out specific cloud‑physics retrievals, and many compare “microwave + GTS” versus “IR + GTS,” making it difficult to attribute every percent to passive microwave alone. Stronger causal estimates would come from OSSEs that toggle only passive microwave imagers (brightness temperatures and cloud/precipitation retrievals) against identical IR/radiosonde controls, and from multi‑year WNP ensembles that quantify percent reductions in 48‑h intensity error attributable solely to microwave imagers. Nonetheless, the direction and magnitude of effect are clear: microwave imagers causally reduce 48‑h typhoon intensity forecast error when held against controls and non‑microwave sources, with typical absolute reductions over 24–48 h on the order of a few knots in maximum wind and double‑digit reductions in SLP and intensity variance, and with combined microwave DA generally outperforming non‑microwave DA over the full 48 h.
