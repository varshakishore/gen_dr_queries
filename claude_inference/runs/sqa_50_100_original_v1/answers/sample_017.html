# Acute pulmonary edema due to fluid overload in dengue fever with warning signs

## What "dengue with warning signs" and severe dengue mean
Dengue patients at risk of deterioration are classified as "dengue with warning signs" when they present with abdominal pain or tenderness, persistent vomiting, clinical fluid accumulation (e.g., edema, pleural or ascites), mucosal bleeding, lethargy/restlessness, hepatomegaly, or a rising hematocrit as hemoconcentration develops.</cite id="b2ab4ba9-5"> Warning signs reflect impending severe plasma leakage and organ involvement and warrant close hospital monitoring.</cite id="b2ab4ba9-7"> Severe dengue is defined by severe plasma leakage leading to shock, fluid accumulation with respiratory distress, severe bleeding requiring clinical intervention, or severe organ involvement (e.g., liver enzymes >1000 U/L, encephalopathy, myocarditis, or renal impairment).</cite id="b2ab4ba9-5"> These criteria are used in contemporary guidance to identify patients who need intensive observation and supportive care.</cite id="b2ab4ba9-7">

## Pathophysiology: plasma leakage, distributive shock, and why fluid overload worsens respiratory failure
The hallmark of severe dengue is plasma leakage from injured microvasculature, driving hypovolemia, shock, and organ dysfunction.</cite id="b2ab4ba9-4"> Early signs include tachycardia and narrowing of pulse pressure as vascular permeability increases.</cite id="b2ab4ba9-5"> Over-resuscitation with isotonic fluids in this setting can worsen plasma leakage and exacerbate fluid accumulation, including pulmonary edema and pleural effusions.</cite id="b2ab4ba9-6"> Excessive fluids during the critical phase have been associated with clinical deterioration and may counteract the benefits of careful fluid management.</cite id="b2ab4ba9-4"> Balancing oxygen delivery to tissues with the risk of capillary overfilling is central to avoiding fluid overload–induced pulmonary edema.</cite id="b2ab4ba9-6">

## Clinical recognition and monitoring
Clinical deterioration in dengue correlates with the progression of fluid leakage rather than platelet counts alone, emphasizing the need to recognize leakage early.</cite id="b2ab4ba9-2"> Patients with warning signs deteriorate rapidly; thus, admission for observation and frequent reassessment is advised.</cite id="b2ab4ba9-5"> Signs of fluid overload include rising edema, ascites/pleural effusions, and respiratory distress; laboratory features include increasing hematocrit with falling platelets as hemoconcentration and bleeding risks evolve.</cite id="b2ab4ba9-4"> During the critical phase, maintaining urine output (e.g., ≥0.5 mL/kg/hour) guides fluid therapy, but excessive fluids can precipitate pulmonary edema.</cite id="b2ab4ba9-5"> Institutions implementing careful fluid titration and monitoring have reported fewer cases of pulmonary edema and third spacing while reducing hospital stay.</cite id="b2ab4ba9-8">

## Management: preventing and treating fluid overload with pulmonary edema

- Initial stabilization and oxygenation: In severe dengue with warning signs, early admission and supportive care (including supplemental oxygen as needed) are recommended to prevent rapid deterioration.</cite id="b2ab4ba9-5"> When respiratory distress develops, escalation to higher-dependency or ICU care with oxygen support and hemodynamic monitoring is indicated.</cite id="b2ab4ba9-6">

- Individualized fluid therapy: Fluid management must be tailored to each patient with frequent reassessment. In warning signs and early shock, isotonic crystalloids are used initially, with the goal of maintaining perfusion while avoiding overload; once stabilized, rates are reduced to maintenance.</cite id="b2ab4ba9-6"> Overhydration should be actively prevented because it can worsen plasma leakage and respiratory compromise.</cite id="b2ab4ba9-6"> Observational data suggest that judicious fluid restriction after initial resuscitation reduces pulmonary edema and third spacing compared with proactive overhydration strategies.</cite id="b2ab4ba9-8">

- Hemodynamic monitoring and de-resuscitation: Patients with fluid overload can present with low cardiac output and high systemic vascular resistance; hemodynamic tools can help detect overload and guide restriction and inotropic support when needed.</cite id="b2ab4ba9-6"> In the ICU, net positive fluid balances may be tolerated early to stabilize hemodynamics, followed by de-resuscitation as vasopressor needs decrease to mitigate fluid overload risk in established respiratory failure.</cite id="b2ab4ba9-3">

- Vasopressors and shock: Persistent hypotension after optimizing fluids requires vasopressors; severe cases may need inotropes and mechanical ventilation if myocarditis or mixed shock complicates illness.</cite id="c162b502-0"> Careful fluid restriction with close monitoring is emphasized to prevent exacerbating pulmonary edema while supporting perfusion with vasopressors.</cite id="c162b502-1">

- Recognizing and managing ARDS/pulmonary edema: When dengue coexists with ARDS, initial resuscitation for perfusion is followed by strict fluid restriction to prevent worsening edema; prone positioning and other ARDS strategies are used when oxygenation is severely impaired.</cite id="c162b502-3"> Observational data in adults demonstrate that overhydration is associated with higher edema and effusions and may blunt pulse pressure despite low shock rates, underscoring the risk of fluid overload even when shock is preventable.</cite id="b2ab4ba9-4">

## Special considerations and complications
Patients with comorbid chronic kidney disease or heart failure face a heightened risk of fluid overload in dengue; management often requires goal-directed resuscitation during shock with a transition to restrictive fluid policies afterward, alongside hemodynamic monitoring (e.g., IVC diameter and collapsibility, lung ultrasound) to individualize decisions.</cite id="c162b502-9"> Fluid overload in the critically ill is linked to worse outcomes across conditions with fluid accumulation, reinforcing the need for early recognition and de-resuscitation strategies in dengue with respiratory compromise.</cite id="c162b502-8"> Cases of severe dengue with fluid overload, encephalopathy, and shock illustrate that early detection of fluid overload and prompt restriction/inotropic support can improve outcomes.</cite id="c162b502-6">

## Practical takeaways
- Classify early: Use WHO warning signs to identify high-risk patients needing hospitalization and close monitoring.</cite id="b2ab4ba9-7">
- Avoid over-resuscitation: Titrate fluids to maintain perfusion while preventing overload; escalate to ICU when respiratory distress or shock develops.</cite id="b2ab4ba9-6">
- Monitor aggressively: Track vital signs, hematocrit, platelets, liver enzymes, urine output, and fluid balance; anticipate fluid accumulation and pulmonary edema in warning-sign patients.</cite id="b2ab4ba9-5">
- Oxygen and ICU care: Provide supplemental oxygen and ICU-level support when respiratory involvement occurs; use hemodynamic monitoring to detect and manage fluid overload.</cite id="b2ab4ba9-6">
- Restrict then de-resuscitate: After stabilizing with fluids and/or vasopressors, shift to restrictive fluid policies and actively remove excess fluid to reduce pulmonary edema risk, especially in ARDS or comorbid populations.</cite id="b2ab4ba9-3">
