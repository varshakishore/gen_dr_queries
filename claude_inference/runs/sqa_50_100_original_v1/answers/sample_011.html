Motor imagery (MI) recruits the same motor cortical circuitry used for movement execution, increasing excitability and plasticity in the contralateral and ipsilateral primary motor cortex (M1) and related nodes such as SMA and premotor cortex; this circuit-level engagement is a prerequisite for MI to drive synaptic and trophic mechanisms that restore motor function after injury or disease.<cite id=95f422bc-2,95f422bc-6,95f422bc-7,95f422bc-5>Motor imagery and motor execution share a similar neural network with M1 and SMA at its core, and MI increases contralateral M1 excitability; MI-based training induces plasticity in ipsilateral M1/PMv that could support skill reacquisition, with neuroimaging showing overlapping M1/SMA/PMC/cerebellar reorganization during MI and physical practice</cite>. Taken together, MI likely promotes motor recovery through a two-pronged mechanism. Current evidence links MI to M1 circuit engagement and BDNF-dependent plasticity, and shows that increased M1 activity can enhance cortical neurogenesis after stroke; however, direct demonstrations that MI increases neurogenesis markers in M1 or hippocampus in humans remain limited.
