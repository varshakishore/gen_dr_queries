  1. [1] Affective polarization and political (dis)trust: Investigating their interconnection and the moderating role of (social) media use
    Annelien Van Remoortere, R. Vliegenthart
    Polarization is part of any political system as it reflects (substantial) disagreements between political elites or voters. In itself polarization is thus not necessarily harmful was it not for findings in recent research that witnessed a shift in attention from substantially driven issue polarization to one that is focused on individuals and translates in a dislike and distrust of those from another (ideologically remote) party. This type of polarization is called affective polarization and is, more so than issue polarization, seen as a threat to democracies and society at large (Iyengar et al., 2019). The rise of affective polarization indicates a shift in society from more opinion centered to individual centered polarization (Törnberg et al., 2021). Affective polarization can be defined as citizens' tendency to positively evaluate their preferred party and its supporters (in-party favorability) while harboring negative sentiments toward the opposing party (out-party animosity) (Gidron et al., 2020;Iyengar et al., 2012). The fear is that affective polarization has more serious implications than issue polarization as highly polarized individuals would be more prone to undemocratic attitudes like rejecting election results and supporting authoritarian leaders (Janssen, 2024;Kingzette et al., 2021). Research on affective polarization largely originated in the bi-partisan system of the United States, mainly focusing on the clear distinction between in-party and out-party. There are, however, also studies on affective polarization in multiparty systems (e.g. Reiljan, 2020;Wagner, 2021) which focus mainly on different forms of antipathy, toward elites and institutions (vertical) or toward other citizens (horizontal) (Harteveld, 2021). Apart from studying the presence and strength of affective polarization, research has also focused on identifying the various causes. A variety of macro-level factors was found that contribute to changes in affective polarization (see e.g. Iyengar et al., 2019;Röllicke, 2023). An often discussed cause is increased partisan sorting (Mason and Davis, 2015) in which partisans increasingly align their ideological and partisan identities (Levendusky 2009).
  2. [2] A New Measure of Affective Polarization
    N. Campos, Christopher M. Federico
    Affective polarization is the tendency to evaluate in-party members positively and out-party members negatively (Iyengar and Westwood 2015). This conceptualization is rooted in social identity theory, which argues that identification with a social group (such as a political party) is sufficient to generate favoritism for that group (Huddy, Bankert, and Davies 2018;Tajfel et al. 1971). According to social identity theory, people want to feel positively about the groups they belong to, which leads them to want the ingroup to do better than or compare favorably to outgroups (Tajfel and Turner 1979). To measure affective polarization, various indices have been used, including trait ratings of the two parties (Iyengar, Sood, and Lelkes 2012;Levendusky 2018;Levendusky and Malhotra 2016a), measures of social distance (Iyengar, Sood, and Lelkes 2012;Klar, Krupnikov, and Ryan 2018), the number of positive and negative things an individual says about the two parties (Levendusky and Malhotra 2016a), and even implicit association tests (Iyengar and Westwood 2015). Most common in the literature, however, is warmth bias. Warmth bias is assessed by taking the difference between ratings of the in-party and the out-party (assessed using thermometer ratings of the Democratic and Republican parties). One of the major advantages of using this measure is that it can be constructed using only two items, with these two items frequently present in long-running, archived surveys such as the American National Election Studies. With data spanning decades, scholars have shown that the electorate has become increasingly polarized compared to previous eras (Iyengar and Krupenkin 2018: Iyengar et al. 2019: Iyengar, Sood, and Lelkes 2012). Though two-party U.S. politics has been a dominant focus of the literature on affective polarization, it has also been studied in nations with multiparty systems. These studies suggest that affective polarization is not exclusive to the U.S. two-party system (Boxell, Gentzkow, and Shapiro 2024;Gidron, Adams, and Horne 2020).
  3. [3] The affective nature of affective polarization: Evidence from physiological and self‐reported responses to US politicians
    Kevin Arceneaux, Bert N. Bakker
    While many scholars use the term "affective polarization" as shorthand to broadly encompass the mass-level phenomenon as well as its individual-level counterpart, it is important to be more precise when formulating hypotheses. Polarization is a sociological construct that occurs at the mass-level. One can speak of the United States as "affectively polarized," but not individuals. The source of this imprecision comes from the fact that both the theoretical construction and operationalization of affective polarization rests on the assumption that polarization is simply an aggregation of the difference in how individuals feel toward members of the inparty and the out-party. Nonetheless, it is important to state explicitly what we are measuring when we study the phenomenon at the individual level. At the individual level, there are three elements that, when considered together and aggregated, serve as an indicator for affective polarization. The first is partisan identity. As a social psychological construct, affective polarization starts with the notion that people categorize themselves and others as members of social groups, creating ingroups (individuals that share one's social group identity) and outgroups (those who share a different social group identity). As a theoretical construct, affective partisan polarization presumes that people have a partisan identity and thus a partisan ingroup and a partisan outgroup. At the individual level, in-party amity coupled with out-party animus is a form of ingroup bias, which, when aggregated, constitutes a measure of affective partisan polarization.

  1. [1] How language divides: Asymmetries in the differentiating power of opinion discourse
    Kevin Durrheim, Davide Morselli, M. Schuld, M. Mafunda, Andrés Martínez Torres, M. Beramendi et al.
    Despite its diversity, research on polarization in social psychology shares a common methodological feature: heavy reliance on survey responses. Affective polarization (Iyengar et al., 2012) is typically measured using instruments such as the "feeling thermometer" (Gidron et al., 2022), while political and opinion polarization are assessed through rating scales capturing attitudes toward social groups or policy positions. Although polarization is "most properly understood as a description of the distribution of a population" (Bakker & Lelkes, 2024, p. 419), it is frequently operationalized as an individual-difference variable. Participants are asked to rate how warm or cold they feel toward ingroups and outgroups, and the difference between these ratings is treated as a proxy for polarization (e.g., Kingzette et al., 2021;Wakefield & Wakefield, 2023). These scores are then used as predictors or outcomes in linear models, abstracting polarization from its social and communicative context. This approach allows researchers to observe patterns of attitudinal congruence and divergence, but it overlooks how people judge group affiliation and opinion similarity in everyday settings (cf. Durrheim, 2022;Durrheim et al., 2016). By eliciting privately expressed attitudes, surveys estimate mental states within a broader distribution, yet they do not examine the identity performances (Klein et al., 2007) through which individuals publicly signal their views and interpret those of others. Such judgments are foundational: they enable actors to perceive alignment and opposition and thereby construct the shared opinion structures that make polarization possible. A deeper problem concerns measurement validity. Research shows that responses to rating scales often fail to map straightforwardly onto real-world expression (Potter, 1998). Survey answers vary with framing and question order (Rockwood et al., 1997), and it is often unclear which targets or dimensions respondents invoke when answering measures of affective polarization (Druckman & Levendusky, 2019). What is measured depends heavily on how categories are constructed and interpreted-processes that remain opaque within the instrument itself. In everyday interaction, individuals contradict themselves, articulate nuanced positions, and adjust their views across contexts.
