## Short answer

The best real-world natural experiment for this question is Ethereum's "Merge" of September 15, 2022, which transitioned the same network—same users, same applications, comparable validator/node population, and identical transaction throughput—from Proof-of-Work to Proof-of-Stake essentially overnight. The Merge was executed on September 15, 2022, completing Ethereum's transition to proof-of-stake consensus, officially deprecating proof-of-work and dropping energy consumption by an estimated 99.95%. ## Why this is close to a clean causal estimate

The Merge is an unusually good identification setting because it isolates the consensus mechanism as the treatment. The Merge did not require users to exchange their tokens for new ones; the contracts were preserved, and it reduced Ethereum's energy consumption by 99.98%. Because the application layer, transaction volume, and the broad set of network participants were held fixed across the switch, the observed energy collapse is attributable to the consensus change itself rather than to a change in usage or scale.

Your two stated controls—validator set size and transaction throughput—actually clarify *why* the effect is so large rather than diminishing it:

- **Throughput is roughly orthogonal to PoW energy use.** Mining energy is governed by hash-rate competition, not by how many transactions are processed. ## Bottom line

Holding validator population and throughput fixed, switching a network from PoW to PoS reduces electricity consumption by roughly **99.95% to 99.99%**—a factor of about **2,000×**. The effect is large and robust precisely because PoW energy use is driven by competitive hash-rate expenditure that is independent of how many transactions are processed, while PoS replaces that brute-force computation with lightweight validation whose energy footprint is dominated by ordinary server overhead.
