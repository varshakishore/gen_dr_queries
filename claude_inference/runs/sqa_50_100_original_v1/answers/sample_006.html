Introduction to OCD and General Treatment Principles

Obsessive-Compulsive Disorder (OCD) is a chronic and often debilitating neuropsychiatric condition characterized by the presence of obsessions (unwanted, intrusive thoughts) and/or compulsions (repetitive behaviors or mental acts felt to be driven) that the person experiences as unacceptable and is performed to reduce anxiety. The disorder has a lifetime prevalence of approximately 1-3% in the general population and can onset at any age, though often begins in adolescence or early adulthood. OCD typically follows a chronic, fluctuating course with periodic remissions and exacerbations, significantly impacting patients' quality of life, work/school performance, social relationships, and overall functioning. <Model name="Anthropic" version="claude-3-7-sonnet-20250219">

The exact etiology of OCD remains unclear, though research suggests a multifactorial inheritance pattern with contributions from genetic, neurobiological, environmental, and psychosocial factors. Neuroimaging studies have identified abnormalities in specific brain circuits, particularly those involving the basal ganglia, prefrontal cortex, and anterior cingulate cortex, while neurotransmitter imbalances—especially involving serotonin—appear to play a role. <Model name="Anthropic" version="claude-3-7-sonnet-20250219">

Treatment for OCD typically involves a combination of psychotherapy and pharmacotherapy, with the specific approach depending on the severity of symptoms, patient preferences, and the presence of comorbid conditions. Evidence-based first-line treatments include selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) and/or clomipramine (a tricyclic antidepressant), as well as cognitive behavioral therapy with exposure and response prevention (CBT-E/RP). For mild cases without significant functional impairment, lower-intensity interventions such as brief CBT or self-help approaches may be appropriate. <Model name="Anthropic" version="claude-3-7-sonnet-20250219">

While standard treatments can be effective for many patients, approximately 20-40% of individuals with OCD do not respond adequately to initial therapies and are considered treatment-resistant. These patients may require more intensive or alternative approaches, including higher-dose SSRIs, combination therapy, augmentation with medications such as antipsychotics, or emerging treatments like deep brain stimulation. <Model name="Anthropic" version="claude-3-7-sonnet-20250219">
