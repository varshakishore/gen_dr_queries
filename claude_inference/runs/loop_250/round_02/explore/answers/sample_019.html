    Even though Indlamu dance has been a central feature of the Zulu nation, few historical studies address the social status of Indlamu. Indlamu has been largely overlooked as an image of Zulu upper-class people, even though the majority of people had ample access to Indlamu. Also, Indlamu is most often associated with Zulu culture untouched by Western influence, probably because it is regarded as a touchstone of Zulu identity. The purpose of this article is to demonstrate that Indlamu has never been about war dance only for the peasants and migrants’ workers (ordinary people), but rather ‘an image of the Zulu upper-class culture of the past’. The demonstration is done by analysing Indlamu’s history, structural form, performance, and appreciation throughout the decades. This paper employs a textual analysis approach and direct interviews with selected participants who possess extensive knowledge of Indlamu.     S. Yende
    It is prudent to foreground this article by giving a brief overview on uMkhosi woMhlanga among amaZulu nation, so that the influence of Zulu traditional music under scrutiny could be contextualised. UMkhosi woMhlanga ceremony, also known as the Zulu Reed Dance or Festival, are annual cultural events celebrated by the amaZulu tribe in South Africa (de Robillard, 2009;Nkosi, 2019;Mchunu, 2020). This ceremony holds a deep cultural and historical significance within Zulu society. uMkhosi woMhlanga as a ceremony often take place in eNyokeni KwanoNgoma during the months of late August or early September (Nkosi, 2019;Mchunu, 2020). uMkhosi woMhlanga serve as a powerful focal point for the amaZulu tribe. This gathering of thousands of young Zulu women from diverse communities is a testament to the enduring cultural importance of the event and the integral role that Zulu traditional music plays in enhancing its significance (de Robillard, 2009;Dlamini, 2021;Phoswa, 2013). The primary purpose of uMkhosi woMhlanga is to celebrate and promote the virtues of virginity, unity, cultural heritage among Zulu women (Nkosi, 2019). Evidently, uMkhosi woMhlanga ceremonies serve as a cultural cornerstone for the amaZulu, offering a platform for these young women to celebrate their collective identity and honour their heritage. Recent research and publication analysis. Scientists point out that during this ceremony, young women, known as "imbali" or "maidens," participate in various activities, including the ceremonial cutting of reeds and the creation of reed bundles (Treffry-Goatley et al., 2018;Nkosi, 2019;Ntuli, 2018). These reeds are then presented to the Zulu king, who receives them as a symbol of purity and the participants' commitment to upholding their virginity (de Robillard, 2009;Nkosi, 2019). The event is a colourful and joyful display of Zulu cultural traditions, featuring traditional attire, dance, and song.
